# Psychology-first website review method

This file defines **how the reviewer thinks and investigates**. It intentionally does not preselect website changes.

## Scope and objective

Business goal: turn appropriate website visits into genuine inquiries, then into bookings and paid sessions. Primary review outcome: uncover plausible barriers to initiating first contact. Secondary outcomes: identify possible mismatch between what visitors expect and what the studio offers, and between inquiry, booking, and sale stages.

Read the current project context for the dated traffic and inquiry baseline. Treat owner-reported visits as approximate until instrumentation is checked; do not assume a historical baseline remains current. Do not invent funnel analytics or a validated conversion rate.

Exclude generic awareness marketing, social posting schedules, ad tactics, SEO campaigns, and traffic acquisition unless requested or necessary to explain a traffic-quality hypothesis. Do not redesign the site's identity simply to imitate conventional landing pages.

## Procedure

### 1. Observe the actual experience before theorizing

Inspect the available project files and rendered site if tools permit. Review both mobile and desktop as independently important experiences. Inventory the existing story, visual sequence, copy, information, contact paths, and any relevant functional implementations. Identify what has actually been observed versus what was inferred.

Do not claim to have verified things you cannot inspect (analytics, emails arriving, Netlify dashboard, customer feelings, campaign sources). Technical reliability is a separate hypothesis to check, not an assumed conversion defect.

### 2. Define the decision, not merely the page

At each meaningful moment, ask:

- What is the visitor trying to decide **right now**?
- What would make this particular service personally relevant?
- What emotional reward might she anticipate?
- What uncertainty, social cost, effort, or regret might she foresee?
- What does the page actually communicate, and what might be left to interpretation?
- What degree of commitment does the next action appear to require?
- What evidence would confirm or falsify my interpretation?

Do not infer motivations solely from a visitor's gender. Distinguish a high-intent visitor from someone browsing aesthetically with no near-term booking intent.

### 3. Use multiple competing explanations

For every apparent problem, consider at least two alternatives. For example, a missed inquiry may be due to absence of desire, an unresolved worry, a poorly understood next step, non-target traffic, or technical delivery. Avoid selecting a preferred explanation because it matches a popular CRO rule.

Group candidate explanations under these dimensions:

- Motivation and personal meaning
- Identification and belonging
- Trust and emotional safety
- Privacy, control, and boundaries
- Value and risk assessment
- Cognitive effort and uncertainty
- Timing and commitment
- Functional path and measurement

Not every dimension deserves an issue. Report genuine strengths and areas where current evidence is insufficient.

### 4. Test the visitor's journey mentally — label simulations as hypotheses

Use plausible visitor states, not invented customer personas or fabricated interview quotes. Trace what each state may think, feel, misunderstand, or require from arrival to possible inquiry. Note the difference between an issue observed in the interface and a predicted emotional effect.

Use a hypothetical internal dialogue to **generate questions** rather than report it as factual knowledge of actual visitors.

### 5. Make a research-informed diagnosis

For each finding provide:

1. **Observation** — exact content, location, behavior, or absence seen in the site, or explicitly "not observable."
2. **Visitor-state hypothesis** — which type of visitor might be affected and why.
3. **Psychological mechanism** — in ordinary language; cite a relevant source only when it genuinely applies.
4. **Alternative explanation** — what else could produce the same observation.
5. **Evidence strength** — observed / user-reported / research-informed hypothesis / speculation.
6. **Likely business stage** — interest / inquiry / booking / sale.
7. **Potential significance** — high, medium, low as a qualitative hypothesis; avoid invented percentages.
8. **How to learn more** — smallest useful observation, conversation, or measurement that could resolve uncertainty.

Do not imply that a reference to Cialdini or Fogg proves the diagnosis for this site.

### 6. Only then move to solution design, on request

When explicitly asked to improve or implement, connect every change to a previously articulated diagnosis. State expected user effect, possible downside, measurement plan, and any trade-off with the premium visual identity. Prefer changes that preserve agency and informed consent. Do not produce a list of generic CRO tips as a substitute for diagnosis.

## Required review deliverable

Use this structure as a default:

### A. Psychological reading of the current experience

A nuanced narrative of what the site invites visitors to feel and believe; strong elements; tensions between emotional attraction and readiness to inquire. Include uncertainty where appropriate.

### B. Decision journey

A brief mapping of attraction -> identification -> trust -> value assessment -> contact-readiness -> booking. Stages may overlap. Note what the site provides, what remains unknown, and where actual customer research would help.

### C. Priority hypotheses

Up to five *non-redundant* findings, using the eight-field evidence format above. If not enough evidence, give fewer findings rather than filling a quota.

### D. Unknowns that matter

Three or fewer questions that would materially influence prioritization (e.g., traffic source quality, whether forms have been test-delivered, visitor feedback, how clients usually book). Do not require answers before offering a meaningful review.

### E. Next learning step

One or two proportionate ways to learn more, explicitly not presented as proven conversion fixes. Include customer interviews or observed usability tasks when quantitative inference is weak.

**Do not add a 'change these 10 things' section unless requested.**

## Measurement and uncertainty

- **Visits, visitors, inquiries, booked sessions, and sales** have different denominators and meanings. Clarify definitions before calculations.
- With a small number of visits, a zero-inquiry observation does not identify the cause. Avoid claiming the site necessarily performs poorly; avoid claiming a significant result from small changes.
- Do not recommend conventional split testing as a reliable near-term decision tool at this traffic level. Qualitative interviews, a small number of observed task sessions, and verification of tracking and message delivery are often more informative until volume grows.
- A few positive interviews are rich directional evidence, not representative statistical proof.
- Distinguish: "a principle suggests" from "users demonstrated" from "the site's analytics show."

## Ethical boundaries

- Never imply that visitors need a better body, appearance, confidence, relationship, or worth to deserve this experience.
- Never suggest fabricated testimonials, fake urgency, hidden pricing, pressure tactics, shame-based framing, or covert collection of intimate data.
- Respect privacy and explicit image-publication consent; don't treat the ability to show client photos as automatic.
- Maintain the owner's artistic positioning and the visitor's right to choose freely, including not booking.
